Undermount or inset sink: which suits a stone worktop

Written by Nick, Managing Director, Topcat Worktops. Last reviewed 14 August 2026

For most kitchens an undermount is the better sink in a stone worktop, because the stone runs straight into the bowl and you can sweep water and crumbs off the top in one movement. An inset, or drop-in, sits on the surface with its rim proud of the stone; it is simpler to cut and it carries its own weight, but it leaves a silicone seam around the whole sink to wipe around. The two things that swing it back to an inset are weight and replacement, which is why a heavy fireclay bowl, or a new sink going into an existing run, is often better dropped in.

How the cutout is made and held

An inset is the simpler piece of work. We cut an aperture a little smaller than the outside of the sink's rim, ease the edge, and the sink drops in from above onto a bead of silicone. The rim covers the cut edge, so that edge never has to be polished, and the sink is held by its own clips pulling up against the underside of the stone.

An undermount is a different job. The aperture is cut to the inside line of the bowl, which means every millimetre of that cut edge is on show, so it is machined and then hand polished in our own workshop to the same finish as the rest of the top, corner radii included. The sink is fixed to the underside on studs resined into the stone, and a heavier bowl gets a cradle built into the cabinet to carry it. On most jobs our fabricators bond the sink to the top on the bench, so the line between stone and steel is set in the workshop rather than on your kitchen floor, and the top arrives as one piece.

There are three ways to set that line. Flush, with the stone finishing level with the inside of the bowl. Negative, with the stone lipping over by a millimetre or two. Positive, set back so a strip of the sink rim shows. We fit most of them slightly negative, because water and crumbs go over the edge into the bowl, and because a positive ledge sits wet and collects limescale in this part of the country.

Where the dirt collects day to day

This is the part people notice after six months rather than on the day. An inset sink has a silicone seam running all the way round the rim, often three or four metres of it, and it sits in the wettest spot in the house. It greys off. It goes black at the back corners where water stands. Every few years someone has to rake it out and redo it, and the joint is right there under the light.

An undermount has a joint too, but it is underneath the stone and water cannot sit on it. Day to day you sweep the board straight into the bowl and the top wipes in one pass, with nothing to work around.

The honest caveat is that a hidden joint fails quietly. Open the cupboard under the sink twice a year and put your hand on the back of the carcass. If it feels damp, say so early and it stays a small job.

When an inset sink is the better answer

A heavy fireclay or ceramic bowl is the clearest case. A big one weighs over 30 kg empty and close to double that full of water and pans. We can undermount one, with a proper cradle under it and studs into the stone, and it will be fine. But a drop-in carries its weight on its own rim, spread across the top of the cabinet, and takes the whole argument off the table for the next twenty years.

Replacement is the other case. If you are putting a new worktop into a kitchen that already works, with the plumbing where it is and the cabinets staying, an inset keeps the job simple and keeps your options open. A drop-in sink can be swapped in an afternoon by anyone. An undermount that has been bonded to the stone means lifting the top out to change it, so choose a sink you will still be happy with in fifteen years.

There are smaller reasons too. A pressed steel sink with a moulded drainer is designed to be inset and looks wrong any other way. If the base cabinets are older and not quite square, a rim covers a good deal. And if you already own a sink you like, keeping it inset means we cut to the sink in front of us rather than to a drawing.

What you pay for on the quote

The difference between the two is labour, not stone. An undermount aperture takes longer on the machine, then a fabricator hand polishes all four edges and the corner radii, then the sink is set, bonded and left to cure. That work appears as its own line, and it is worth asking any company to show it as its own line, so you can weigh it against everything else you are spending on the room.

Drainer grooves are the extra most people ask about, and the one we most often talk people out of. Water is hard across London and the home counties. Grooves collect limescale, they dull before the rest of the top does, and they need a brush rather than a cloth. A drainer rack you can lift and dry, or a sink with a moulded drainer, stays looking right for longer, and that money does more good spent on the sink itself.

One thing to be straight about with marble and quartzite: the run around a sink is the wettest, hardest working piece of stone in the kitchen, and marble will etch there from lemon, wine and limescale remover however carefully it is sealed. It can still be the right choice if you want that patina and accept it. Marble, quartzite and granite are priced on application, once we have seen the slab and your layout.

No sink on site, no template

Your sink and your tap have to be on site, out of their boxes, before we template. The templater works from the sink itself, sitting on the cabinets, in your kitchen. That is how the aperture, the corner radii, the tap holes and the reveal all end up right.

Manufacturer drawings are not always accurate, and an error of a millimetre or two that would vanish under an inset rim is visible forever on a flush or negative reveal. If the sink turns up after template day, we either come back or we cut to paper, and neither is as good as cutting to the thing itself.

Two more things for that visit. An undermount usually needs the top front rail of the base unit cut down so the sink body fits, so tell whoever is building the kitchen before they build it. And the waste position, a disposal unit if you are having one, any extra tap hole for filtered water, and the hob if it sits in the same run, all get set on the same day.

If you are still weighing it up, work it out with the plan in front of you. The home visit is free, we will look at the cabinets and at the sink you are considering, and we will tell you when the more expensive answer is the wrong one.
