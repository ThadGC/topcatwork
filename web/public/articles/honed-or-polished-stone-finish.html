Honed or polished: which stone finish is right for your worktop

Written by Nick, Managing Director, Topcat Worktops. Last reviewed 31 July 2026

Polished is the more forgiving finish in a busy kitchen, and honed is the better looking one in a quiet, well lit room. Polished reflects light, so it deepens colour, hides dust and wipes clean with a cloth. Honed absorbs light, so it looks calmer and more natural, but it shows fingerprints, water marks and everyday smears far more readily on dark stone. Neither is right or wrong; the answer depends on the colour of the slab, the light in the room and how hard the kitchen gets used.

What the finish actually does to the stone

Both finishes are the same material. The difference is how far the surface has been taken through the polishing heads. A honed surface is stopped early, leaving a flat matt face that scatters light. A polished surface is taken all the way, leaving a glassy face that reflects it. Nothing is added and nothing is coated; it is the same stone with a different level of smoothness.

That changes what you see. Polishing pulls the colour up and adds depth, so a black granite goes properly black and the veining in a marble reads sharp and wet looking. Hone the same slab and it lightens by a noticeable amount, greys off slightly, and the veining softens. People are often surprised by how different two offcuts of one slab look side by side.

This is why a colour you liked on a website can disappoint in the kitchen. You were looking at a polished sample and you ordered honed, or the other way round. Always judge the finish and the colour together, on a piece of the actual material, not on a photograph.

Fingerprints, water marks and daily use

Here is the part that talks a lot of people out of honed. On dark stone, a honed surface shows fingerprints and hand oils clearly, because the matt face holds the residue where a polished face lets it sit on top and wipe off. Every palm print on the edge of the island near the fridge will be visible in the right light. It cleans off easily enough with hot water and a drop of washing up liquid, but it needs doing more often.

Water marks behave the same way. A wet glass left on honed dark stone leaves a ring you can see until the surface dries fully, and hard water in this part of the country leaves a faint chalky mark behind. Polished stone dries clear and shows almost nothing. Dust is the reverse: it sits invisibly on honed stone and shows up as a haze on high gloss black.

Light colours change the maths entirely. A honed white or pale grey quartz is one of the most practical surfaces you can put in a family kitchen, because there is not enough contrast for prints and marks to register. If you want honed and you have young children, go light. If you want honed in a dark colour, go in knowing you will be wiping it down most days.

How the light in the room decides it

Walk into the kitchen at the times you actually use it and look at where the light comes from. A polished surface acts like a mirror. Under a run of spotlights it will throw hot spots back at you, and directly under a big rooflight it can glare enough to be uncomfortable while you are chopping. In a north facing room with one window, that same reflectivity is a gift; it bounces what little light there is around and stops a dark worktop swallowing the room.

Honed does the opposite. It takes the harshness out of downlights and gives an even, quiet surface with no reflection. In a bright extension with glass on two sides it looks superb. In a small dim galley kitchen with a dark slab, it can feel heavy and flat.

The other thing to look at is what the worktop sits under. If you have a wall of gloss handleless units, a honed top gives the eye somewhere to rest. If the units are matt painted timber, a polished top adds the bit of life the room would otherwise be missing. It is worth standing in the space at nine in the morning and again at seven at night before you commit.

Where each finish earns its place

Quartz takes both finishes well and is where most of these decisions get made. Polished quartz is the workhorse: hard, non-porous, reflective enough to shrug off daily marks, and the safe answer for a kitchen with children and a lot of traffic. Honed quartz is the one people fall for in a photograph, and in a pale colour it lives up to it.

Granite is usually polished, and for good reason. The polish is what brings out the crystal in the stone; honed granite can look strangely dull unless the slab has real movement in it. Marble is the opposite case. A lot of people prefer marble honed, because the matt surface disguises the light etching that acids cause over the years, where a polished marble shows every ring from a lemon or a splash of wine as a dull patch. Neither finish makes marble bulletproof, but honed ages more gracefully.

There is also the question of feel. Honed is soft and dry under the hand and grips a chopping board slightly. Polished is cool and slick. It sounds like a small thing until you have lived with it, and it is the reason some people choose a honed island and a polished perimeter run in the same kitchen.

Finish is chosen at the slab, not afterwards

This is the point worth being firm about. The finish is put on the whole slab when the stone is first processed, long before it reaches us, and it is not something you change your mind about once the worktop is templated, cut and fitted. There is no coating we can put on to turn honed into polished, and taking a polished top back to honed on site is not realistic work; it would mean grinding a fitted surface in your kitchen, and the result would not match the edges or the finish you started with.

So treat the finish as a permanent decision made at the same moment you choose the colour, because in practice it is one decision, not two. Look at both finishes in the same material, in daylight, and preferably a large piece rather than a small sample. A hundred millimetre chip tells you almost nothing about how a four metre run will read.

When we come out for the free home visit, we look at the light in the room, the colour of the units and the way the kitchen gets used, and we will say plainly if we think the finish you have in mind is going to annoy you. Our experienced fabricators cut and finish every worktop in our own workshop, so we see both finishes in every material week after week, and we know which choices people are still happy with two years on. That advice costs you nothing, and it is a great deal easier to give before the slab is cut than after.
