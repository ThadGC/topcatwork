How to clean a quartz worktop, and what to keep off it

Written by Nick, Managing Director, Topcat Worktops. Last reviewed 25 August 2026

Clean a quartz worktop with warm water, a soft cloth and a drop of mild washing up liquid, then dry it off. That is genuinely it, on almost every day of its life. Quartz is non-porous, so it never needs sealing, and the damage we get called back out to look at is almost never a stain; it is a heat mark from something hot put straight down on the surface.

Your everyday clean takes about a minute

Warm water, a soft cloth, a small amount of mild washing up liquid. Wipe the surface down, rinse the cloth, go over it again with clean water, then dry it with a tea towel or kitchen roll. Drying is the step most people skip and it is the one that matters most, because it is what keeps a dark quartz looking even rather than streaky.

Do it at the end of cooking, while things are still warm and before anything has set hard. Most of what we see dulling a worktop after five years is not damage at all. It is a thin limescale film from water left to dry on the surface, and in the hard water we get across London, Hertfordshire and Essex that film builds faster than people expect.

A microfibre cloth is worth keeping under the sink. A washing up sponge is fine on the soft side. The green scouring side is not, and that matters more than it sounds.

The products that ruin a quartz surface

Keep these off it completely: bleach and any bleach-based spray, oven cleaner, hob cleaner, drain unblocker, paint stripper, nail varnish remover, methylated spirit, descaler and anything sold on how strong its acid or alkali is. Add dishwasher tablets used as a scrubbing block, a trick that goes round online and takes the sheen off polished quartz permanently.

Quartz is roughly ninety per cent crushed stone and mineral, held together with a polymer resin. The stone does not care about any of those chemicals. The resin does. Once the resin at the surface has been etched or dulled you have a patch that catches the light differently, and it does not come back. That is why a splash of oven cleaner is a far bigger problem on quartz than a glass of red wine.

Abrasives are the other half of it. No scouring pads, no wire wool, no cream cleaner with grit in it, no melamine sponge on a polished finish. They leave fine scratches you will not notice on the day. You notice them in January, when low sun comes across the worktop at an angle and lights up every one of them.

You do not need a specialist quartz cleaner. They sell well and they do very little that warm soapy water does not already do. If you want one thing beyond washing up liquid, keep a mild spray that says it is safe on stone and free of bleach and ammonia, and use it once a week rather than every day.

Lifting a dried-on spill without scratching

Soak it, do not scrape it. Lay a warm damp cloth over the dried patch and leave it there for five or ten minutes while you do something else. Jam, dried gravy, porridge, toothpaste and candle wax all soften and lift with almost no pressure once they have been properly wetted.

If something still will not move, use a plastic scraper or the edge of an old bank card held at a shallow angle. A wooden spatula works too. Never a kitchen knife, never a metal scourer, never a paint scraper blade. The lump of dried food is temporary. The mark you make taking it off in a hurry is not.

There is one exception to the solvent rule. For a sticky adhesive residue or a stubborn bit of wax, a small amount of white spirit on a cloth is safe on quartz for a minute or two. Wash the area properly with soapy water afterwards and dry it. Do not leave it sitting, and keep it off the silicone joints at the wall and around the sink, because those it will attack.

Why quartz never needs sealing

Quartz is non-porous. There is no network of open pores for liquid to travel into, so there is nothing for a sealer to fill and nothing to top up each year. If anyone offers to seal your quartz worktop, or sells you a bottle to do it yourself, that is money going nowhere.

This is the real practical difference between quartz and a natural stone. Marble, quartzite and granite are quarried in one piece and do have pores, so they are sealed and the seal is looked after over time. Quartz is engineered from crushed stone and resin, and the resin closes the surface as the slab is made. Oil, coffee, wine and beetroot sit on top of it rather than soaking in.

That does not quite make it stainproof in the way the word suggests. Leave turmeric, hair dye or strong food colouring on a pale quartz overnight and you can end up with a shadow that takes real work to lift. Wipe the same spill up on the day and you will never know it happened. Quartz forgives anything you clean up reasonably promptly, which covers almost everything a kitchen throws at it.

Heat is the one risk worth planning for

Heat is what actually ruins quartz worktops, and it is the thing homeowners worry about least. The resin at the surface starts to suffer well below the temperature of a pan lifted straight off a gas hob. A hot roasting tin, an air fryer venting downwards, a slow cooker left in the same spot for eight hours: any of those can leave a dull white ring or a fine crack, and no amount of cleaning will touch it.

There is no proper repair for a heat mark. We can sometimes improve one. We cannot make it disappear, and on a run with a visible joint it is rarely worth cutting a new piece for a single ring. So the answer is a trivet or a thick wooden board, every time, with no exception made for a pan you are fairly sure has cooled down. Two minutes off the heat is not cooled down.

The risk nobody plans for is thermal shock at a cut out. A sink or hob cut out is the narrowest point in any worktop, and that is where a sudden temperature difference does damage. Tipping a pan of boiling pasta water into a cold ceramic sink, or standing a hot pan on the strip between hob and sink, is asking a lot of a thin piece of stone. Run the hot tap for a few seconds first and most of that risk goes away.

If you are still choosing and you cook hard on a gas hob, say so before you commit.
