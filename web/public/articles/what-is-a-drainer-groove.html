What is a drainer groove, and do you actually need one

Written by Nick, Managing Director, Topcat Worktops. Last reviewed 17 July 2026

A drainer groove is a shallow channel cut into the surface of a stone worktop beside the sink, so water runs off a washed pan and back into the bowl instead of sitting on the stone. They are usually cut in sets of three to five, machined on a CNC bench in our own workshop before the top ever reaches your house. That timing matters: grooves are decided at template stage, and they cannot be added once the worktop is fitted.

How a drainer groove is machined

The grooves are cut with a diamond profiling wheel on a CNC bench, with water running over the stone the whole time. A typical set is three to five channels, each around 10mm wide and 5mm deep, spaced roughly 70mm to 90mm apart, with rounded ends so there is no sharp stop for dirt to sit in. The wheel leaves the groove matt, so each one is worked back by hand until it matches the finish on the rest of the top.

The stone decides how the groove behaves afterwards. Quartz cuts cleanly and polishes back close to the original surface. Granite is harder on tooling but takes a good polish inside the channel. Marble and quartzite are softer and more open, so a groove in them will mark and etch sooner than the flat surface around it, which is worth knowing before you commit to the idea.

Grooves also need room. We want around 400mm to 600mm of clear worktop beside the bowl for a set that is any real use, and the outer groove has to stay roughly 60mm back from the front edge, clear of any joint, socket cut-out or support rail underneath. If the run beside your sink is shorter than that, we will say so at template rather than cut something that only half works.

Grooves and a recessed drainer board are different things

A drainer groove is a channel cut into a flat surface. A recessed drainer, sometimes called a dished or milled drainer, is a shallow tray milled into the stone, usually 4mm to 6mm deep with a radiused edge all round, often with grooves cut inside it. The recess holds the water rather than simply guiding it, so nothing escapes onto the rest of the top.

It is a far bigger machining job and it takes material. A recess wants 30mm stone, or a 20mm top with a laminated edge, because you are milling depth out of the slab and still need strength left behind. That is why a recessed drainer costs considerably more than a set of grooves, and why it has to be settled early. It changes the thickness of stone you order, not just the machining time.

In practice most kitchens get grooves. The recess suits a deep ceramic sink and a heavy hand-washing routine, or a room where the sink sits under a window and there is nowhere sensible to stand a rack.

How much fall the grooves need before they drain

Grooves cut dead level do not drain. They hold a thin film of water and it stays there until it evaporates, which is how a brand new worktop ends up with limescale lines in it. The set has to fall towards the bowl, and about 1 in 100 is the working minimum, roughly 5mm of drop across a 500mm drainer.

There are two ways to get that fall. Either the grooves are machined progressively deeper as they approach the bowl, or the whole drainer area is cut on a slight slope. Either way it only exists relative to the worktop itself, so if the base units run downhill away from the sink you have cancelled it out. Our fitters level the run and check it before the template goes back to the workshop.

The sink type matters more than most people expect. With an undermount bowl the water runs off the stone and straight over the polished edge into the sink, which is exactly what grooves are for. With an inset sink the rim sits proud of the stone, so the water reaches the rim and stops, and the grooves have to finish short with a small dam at the end of them. If you are set on an inset sink, grooves do much less for you, and we would rather say that before you pay for them.

Why the grooves are cut in the workshop, not in your kitchen

Everything is cut before the top leaves us. The template gives the exact position of the bowl, the taps and the joints, the grooves are set out from that, cut wet on the bench, then polished and checked under a light with the piece lying flat. Doing it in your kitchen is not an option. It is a wet job that throws slurry everywhere and needs the stone clamped flat with the machine bolted down.

So the decision belongs to the template stage, not to fitting day. Once the top is in, sealed and jointed, grooves cannot be added without taking the piece back out, and in most cases that means a new piece of stone rather than an alteration. It costs nothing to talk it through at the free home visit and confirm it when we template, so ask then.

The upside of cutting in the workshop is that anything that goes wrong, a chip at the end of a groove or a run that polishes unevenly, happens where we can deal with it. You never see it, and your kitchen never becomes a workshop.

When a draining rack is the better answer

Most people do not need grooves. If you have a dishwasher and you hand wash a few pans a week, a rack over the sink or a folding mat does the same work, and it lifts away when you want the worktop back. Grooves take up 500mm of the best space in the kitchen permanently, and that stretch beside the sink is where most of the chopping and stacking actually happens.

The water across most of the counties we work in is hard, and grooves are where limescale shows first. On a dark polished stone you will see white lines within weeks unless they are wiped and dried, and on a honed or matt finish the groove floor goes dull faster than the surface around it. That is not a fault, it is what a channel of standing water does over time.

Grooves earn their place when you genuinely wash by hand every day, when there is a deep ceramic sink and no dishwasher, or when a rack would sit in front of a window and spoil the view. That is a real number of kitchens, just not most of them. If you are unsure, leave them off, live with a rack for six months and see whether you miss them. You cannot uncut a groove, but you can always buy a rack.
