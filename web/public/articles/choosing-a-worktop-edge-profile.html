Choosing a worktop edge profile

Written by Nick, Managing Director, Topcat Worktops. Last reviewed 20 August 2026

For most kitchens the right edge is a pencil round or a small bevel, and on a standard 20mm or 30mm slab either one is included in the fabrication. The decision that changes the room is thickness rather than shape: a mitred edge built up to 60mm or 100mm reads as a far heavier worktop and takes many times the workshop time. Either way, the edge is cut once, at template stage, and it cannot be altered after the top is in.

The four profiles worth knowing

A pencil round is the default and it earns that. The top and bottom arris are taken off to a small radius, about 3mm, so the edge is soft to lean against and there is no sharp point waiting to chip. On a 30mm top you barely register it as a shape at all. You just see a clean line of stone.

A full bullnose takes the whole front face round in a half circle. It belongs in traditional kitchens, alongside painted in-frame doors and a deep ceramic sink. Two things to know before you pick it. It makes a 30mm top read thinner, because the eye loses the flat face. And on a stone with strong movement the pattern stretches as it wraps the curve, which can look wrong on a heavily veined quartz.

A bevel, sometimes called a chamfer, is a flat cut across the top arris, usually 5mm to 10mm at 45 degrees. It catches light along the whole run and gives a crisp shadow line that a pencil round does not. Keep it small. A 10mm bevel on a 20mm top leaves very little flat face underneath, and the edge starts to look thin.

A mitred edge is different in kind. Two pieces of stone are cut at 45 degrees and joined so the front face falls as one continuous piece, which is how a 20mm slab becomes a top that reads 60mm, 100mm or 120mm. A shark nose is the same construction with the underside of the return tapered back, so the edge reads like a blade rather than a block. Both are by a wide margin the most work in the shop.

Thickness changes the room more than shape

Most people arrive worrying about pencil round versus bevel. Stand in a finished kitchen and you will struggle to tell those two apart from two metres away. What anyone can tell apart instantly is 20mm from 30mm, and 30mm from a mitred 100mm. Thickness is the decision that carries the room.

A 20mm top reads light and contemporary and suits a flat slab kitchen. A 30mm top is the usual choice here and looks substantial without announcing itself. A mitred 60mm adds real weight. At 100mm and 120mm you are no longer fitting a worktop, you are turning the island into a piece of furniture.

The mistake we see most often is chunky everywhere. When the perimeter and the island are both 100mm, nothing stands out, and the room feels heavy and lower than it actually is. Pick one. A 30mm perimeter with a pencil round and a 100mm mitre on the island reads deliberate, and it puts the money where people actually stand.

There is a measurement to check before you commit to a deep mitred edge. A 100mm apron takes 70mm more out of the opening below than a 30mm top does. On an island with deep pan drawers, an integrated wine cabinet or an appliance with a fixed aperture height, that 70mm has to come from somewhere. Resolve it at template, not on fitting day.

Matching the edge to the kitchen you already have

Traditional and in-frame doors take a bullnose or a generous pencil round on 30mm, and a small bevel works well against painted timber because it picks up the same light as the door mouldings. A flat slab handleless kitchen wants nothing decorative at all. Its whole language is flat planes and shadow gaps, and a rounded front face argues with every one of them. A full bullnose next to a handleless door is the most common mismatch we are asked to fix, and by then it cannot be fixed.

Material has a say too. Marble and quartzite are softer at the arris and will chip more readily than granite or quartz, so a soft pencil round is kinder to them than a crisp bevel, particularly on the corner nearest a doorway or a bin.

Porcelain is a special case. It comes as a thin slab, so a mitre is the only way to give it any apparent thickness. With porcelain the mitred edge is not a style choice, it is simply how the top is built, and the quote reflects that from the start.

Where the workshop time actually goes

A pencil round or a bevel is one pass on the edge polisher. The machine does the shape, our own workshop finishes the ends and the cutouts by hand, and the profile does not move the price of the top.

A mitre is a different job entirely. The slab is cut oversize, the 45 is cut and dressed, the faces are dry fitted, then bonded with colour matched resin, clamped and left, and the whole front face is polished back until the join disappears. On a three metre island with a return at each end, that is most of a day for two of our experienced fabricators. That is what a mitred edge costs you, and it is why it appears as a real line on a quote when a pencil round does not.

Ask what the joint will do in your particular stone. On plain colours a good mitre is invisible. On a dark polished granite the line can catch light at a low angle. On heavily veined stone the vein has to be carried round the corner so the pattern continues down the face, which is the best looking edge in the trade when it is done properly and obvious when it is not. Ask to see photographs of mitres we have run in the material you are choosing.

The one decision you cannot undo

Handles, paint, taps and lighting can all be changed after the kitchen is in. The edge is cut into the stone at fabrication and after that it is fixed. Going from a 30mm pencil round to a mitred 60mm is not an alteration, it is a new worktop, because there is no way to put stone back.

Reducing is barely better. A pencil round can in theory be taken down to a bevel in situ, but on site polishing does not match a factory polish on quartz or granite, and you will see the difference along the run in daylight. Treat the profile as permanent, because it is.

So look at it properly before template. Ask for an offcut with the actual profile run on it, in your actual stone, then hold it at worktop height, about 900mm, and look down at it the way you will every day for fifteen years. Profiles that look dramatic in your hand at eye level all but vanish at 900mm. That one test settles most of these arguments, and it is a large part of why pencil round and bevel are still the two we fit most.
