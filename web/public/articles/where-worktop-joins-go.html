Where worktop joins go, and how we decide the position

Written by Nick, Managing Director, Topcat Worktops. Last reviewed 24 July 2026

A join goes where the slab runs out and where the cut is least visible, which in most kitchens means through the sink or hob cutout rather than the middle of an open run. Slabs are a fixed size, so any worktop longer than about three metres needs a join somewhere. The position is decided at template, standing in your kitchen, not drawn up in an office.

The real constraint is slab size

Stone arrives as slabs, not rolls. A typical quartz slab is a little over three metres long and around 1.4 metres deep, and some run closer to 3.2 by 1.6. Natural stone varies more because it is cut from a block, so the length is whatever that block gave. Anything longer than the slab has to be two pieces. There is no way round it.

Depth matters as much as length. A standard run is 600mm to 650mm deep, so you get two runs across the depth of a slab with little left over, and a 900mm deep island takes the slab in one strip. That is why an island can push a job into a second slab even when the total area looks modest on paper.

Access is the constraint people forget. A 20mm quartz top three metres long and 650mm deep weighs close to 90kg. It has to come off the van, through the door, round the hall, sometimes up a staircase with a half landing. Where a piece will not turn the corner, the join is decided by the house, not by the drawing.

Why a join lands at the sink or the hob

Given a free choice, we put a join through a cutout. A sink opening removes most of the worktop depth, so all that is left of the join is the front rail, usually 80mm to 100mm, and the strip behind the bowl, 50mm to 60mm. Two short lines instead of one continuous 650mm line across open stone.

The same logic applies at a hob. Both of those positions also have a cabinet wall directly underneath, which supports the join along its whole length. A join floating over a dishwasher or an appliance gap has nothing under it and becomes the weak point in the run.

Corners are the other natural place. An L-shaped run has to change direction somewhere, and a join at the corner reads as part of the shape rather than an interruption. On an island we keep the join off the seating side where we can, so nobody is looking down at it while they eat.

What we will not do is drop a join into the middle of a long clear run for our own convenience. If open stone is the only option, we place it where a wall unit or the natural sight line takes your eye elsewhere, and we tell you exactly where it will be before anything is cut.

How the join is made in our own workshop

Both edges are cut dead straight and square. That is most of the job. A join is only ever as good as the two faces meeting, so the cut is machine made in our own workshop, never trimmed on site with a grinder.

Our experienced fabricators mix the resin against an offcut of your own slab rather than a colour chart. Two-part resin, pigment added, tested, adjusted, tested again until the cured line disappears into the stone. On a strongly patterned quartz the resin is matched to the background colour and the speckle carries the rest.

On veined stone we book-match where the slab allows, so the vein runs on across the join instead of stopping dead at it. That decision is made when the pieces are laid out on the slab for cutting, which is why we lay out for the look rather than nesting the pieces for the least waste.

On site the two halves are dry-fitted first, then pulled together with suction clamps, resin squeezed through the seam, the excess taken off before it hardens and the line polished flush. It cures in around half an hour. Keep weight off it for the rest of the day and the job is finished.

What a good join looks and feels like

Run a fingernail across it. You should feel nothing. Level is not negotiable, because if one side sits proud you will catch it every time you wipe the surface, and the lip will collect grease and crumbs.

The line itself should be about the width of a pencil mark, and in most lights you should have to look for it. Be realistic about the stone, though. A busy patterned quartz hides a join almost completely. A plain white, a flat light grey and a dark gloss are the hardest, because there is no pattern to break the eye and the resin line has nowhere to hide.

You may see it more in low raking light, a window at one end of the run or a spotlight sitting directly above it. That is normal and it is not a fault. A fault is a step you can feel, a gap that takes a fingernail, a resin line that has gone noticeably lighter or darker than the stone, or a join that wanders rather than running straight.

Asking for no joins can cost you more than it saves

It is the most common request we get and it is often the wrong one. Going joinless on a run over three metres means a larger slab, more offcut and usually a second slab in the job. You end up paying for stone that goes in the skip.

There is a handling risk as well. Stone is strong in compression and weak in bending, and the narrow bridge in front of a sink cutout is where it breaks. The longer the piece, the more it flexes on the way in. A top that cracks in the hallway costs you weeks, not just money.

Then there is repair. If one piece runs the length of the kitchen and something goes wrong in five years, the whole run comes out. Sectioned sensibly, only the damaged piece is replaced.

A join is not a compromise when it is put in the right place and made properly. Point it at a cutout, sit it over a cabinet wall, colour match it against your own slab and it stops being something you notice. Bring an opinion to the templating by all means; we will walk the kitchen with you at the free home visit and show you where every line is going to land.
